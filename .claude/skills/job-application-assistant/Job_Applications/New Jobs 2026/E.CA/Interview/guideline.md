## Case Study Preparation

### The first response to any case

Do not begin by naming an econometric method. First clarify:

- Who is the client or instructing party?
- What decision, allegation, or legal question must be addressed?
- What is the relevant conduct or transaction?
- Which products, customers, competitors, geography, and period may be affected?
- What is the proposed theory of harm or efficiency?
- What would likely happen in the relevant counterfactual?
- What data are available, at what level of aggregation, and from which sources?
- What must be delivered, to whom, and by when?

Then state a structure aloud:

> I would approach this in five steps: clarify the economic and legal question, formulate the theory of harm and counterfactual, identify the relevant market and competitive constraints, select evidence and methods that can distinguish between explanations, and test the robustness and limitations before translating the result into a clear recommendation.

### General case framework

#### 1. Define the question

Convert a broad concern into a testable question. Examples:

- Would the merger materially reduce competition relative to the no-merger counterfactual?
- Did the alleged cartel raise prices, and what loss followed?
- Does a dominant firm's conduct foreclose equally efficient rivals or harm consumers?
- Is an observed outcome caused by the conduct, or by demand, cost, quality, or macroeconomic changes?

#### 2. Develop the theory of harm and alternatives

State the mechanism, required conditions, and observable implications. Also state plausible alternative explanations. Avoid treating correlation as proof of the alleged mechanism.

#### 3. Define the counterfactual

The counterfactual is often the centre of the analysis:

- Merger: market outcome without the transaction
- Cartel damages: prices and quantities absent the infringement
- Abuse: market outcome without the challenged conduct
- Regulation or state aid: outcome under an alternative policy or without the intervention

#### 4. Assess market context

Consider demand substitution, supply substitution, geographic scope, customer segments, concentration, entry and expansion, buyer power, capacity, innovation, network effects, multi-homing, switching costs, and vertical relationships. Market shares are a starting point, not a complete competitive assessment.

#### 5. Match evidence to the mechanism

Possible evidence includes transaction-level prices and quantities, bids, costs, margins, internal documents, customer switching, diversion, capacity data, entry histories, contracts, competitor information, surveys, and natural experiments.

#### 6. Choose proportionate methods

Begin with data validation and descriptive evidence. Add econometrics or simulation only when it improves identification or decision-making. Explain assumptions and limitations in plain language.

#### 7. Stress-test the conclusion

Check alternative samples, time windows, controls, market definitions, functional forms, clustering levels, placebo periods, outliers, missing data, pre-trends, and sensitivity to key assumptions.

#### 8. Communicate the answer

Conclude with:

- What the evidence supports
- What remains uncertain
- Which assumptions matter most
- What additional evidence would be most valuable
- The practical implication for the case

### Merger case checklist

For a horizontal merger:

1. Identify overlaps and customer groups.
2. Examine closeness of competition, diversion, differentiation, shares, concentration, capacity, and entry.
3. Specify unilateral and, where relevant, coordinated-effects theories.
4. Consider countervailing factors such as entry, expansion, buyer power, repositioning, and efficiencies.
5. Use appropriate tools such as price-concentration analysis, diversion evidence, UPP/GUPPI, demand estimation, bidding analysis, or merger simulation.
6. Test whether results change under plausible market definitions and assumptions.

For digital or platform markets, also discuss network effects, data advantages, zero-price services, ecosystem effects, multi-homing, tipping, interoperability, innovation, and potential competition. Do not assume that network effects automatically imply harm; explain the mechanism and evidence required.

### Cartel damages checklist

1. Define the affected products, customers, geography, and infringement period.
2. Choose a damages framework and counterfactual price.
3. Consider before-and-after, yardstick, difference-in-differences, cost-based, or simulation approaches.
4. Control for demand, input costs, product mix, seasonality, macroeconomic conditions, and other price determinants.
5. Examine anticipation, transition, and lingering effects around the infringement period.
6. Address volume effects, pass-on, interest, and data coverage when relevant.
7. Report uncertainty and sensitivity rather than only one point estimate.

This is especially relevant to Anselm Mattes's and Niklas Gebhard's experience in cartel and information-exchange damages matters.

### Abuse-of-dominance checklist

1. Assess dominance and competitive constraints.
2. Define the conduct precisely: pricing, exclusivity, tying, refusal to supply, self-preferencing, discrimination, or another mechanism.
3. Explain how the conduct could foreclose rivals or exploit customers.
4. Determine whether an as-efficient-competitor analysis, profitability test, switching analysis, or other empirical method is suitable.
5. Consider objective justification, efficiencies, and less restrictive alternatives.
6. Link any foreclosure finding to likely consumer harm, not merely disadvantage to a competitor.

### Practice case: merger of two digital-service providers

**Prompt:** Two providers of a digital communications service plan to merge. They have high combined shares, but the parties argue that competition remains strong and that combining networks will improve quality. How would I assess the merger?

**Structured answer:**

1. Clarify whether services are differentiated, whether one or both sides of a platform are involved, which customer groups overlap, and the geographic scope.
2. Formulate unilateral-effects concerns: loss of close competition may raise price, reduce quality, slow innovation, or worsen non-price terms. Consider whether network effects or data accumulation strengthen the merged firm's position.
3. Examine switching patterns, diversion ratios, customer-level usage, churn, win-loss or bidding data, pricing, quality, capacity, and internal strategy documents.
4. Assess whether competitors can expand, customers can multi-home or switch, new firms can enter, and interoperability limits lock-in.
5. Evaluate claimed efficiencies for merger specificity, verifiability, timing, and pass-through to consumers. Capacity expansion or improved service quality should be quantified where possible.
6. Select methods according to the data: descriptive switching analysis, demand estimation, UPP/GUPPI, quality-adjusted comparisons, event evidence, or a transparent simulation.
7. Present conclusions separately for price, quality, innovation, and different customer groups; show sensitivity to uncertain diversion and efficiency assumptions.

**Connection to my background:** My ICT research helps me reason about network effects, capacity investment, strategic interaction, and heterogeneous consumers. I should use that knowledge to generate relevant hypotheses, while remaining open to the standard tools and evidence required in a real merger review.

## Methods Related to the Role

### Market definition and competitive constraints

Be ready to explain:

- Demand-side and supply-side substitution
- Hypothetical monopolist or SSNIP logic
- Critical-loss analysis and its limitations
- Diversion ratios and closeness of competition
- Geographic and customer-segment differences
- Why market definition and competitive-effects analysis should inform each other

Key caution: observed switching or price correlations may reflect common shocks rather than substitution. Evidence should be triangulated.

### Difference-in-differences and event studies

A concise explanation:

> Difference-in-differences compares the change in an affected group with the contemporaneous change in a suitable comparison group. The central identifying assumption is that, absent the event or conduct, the groups would have followed parallel trends. I would examine pre-trends, treatment timing, composition changes, spillovers, anticipation, serial correlation, and sensitivity to alternative controls and time windows.

Use cases include cartel overcharge estimation, policy changes, entry or exit, and some merger retrospectives. If treatment timing varies, avoid relying mechanically on a simple two-way fixed-effects specification.

### Demand estimation and merger simulation

Explain at a high level:

- Estimate how consumers substitute among differentiated products.
- Use price, quantity or share, product characteristics, and market-level variation.
- Address price endogeneity with a credible source of exogenous variation or instruments.
- Translate estimated demand into diversion, elasticities, mark-ups, and counterfactual outcomes.
- Make supply-side and conduct assumptions explicit.
- Test sensitivity because conclusions can depend heavily on model specification and instruments.

Do not claim expertise with a particular discrete-choice estimator unless prepared to derive and implement it.

### UPP and GUPPI

Be able to explain the intuition:

> After a merger, some sales lost by one product following a price increase are recaptured by the partner product. That internalised diversion creates upward pricing pressure. UPP or GUPPI combines diversion and margins, and may incorporate efficiencies, to screen the strength of that incentive. It is informative but not a complete prediction because it does not by itself capture all competitor responses, entry, repositioning, or dynamic effects.

### Cartel overcharge estimation

Possible approaches:

- Before-and-after comparison
- Yardstick or comparator market
- Difference-in-differences
- Cost-based or forecasting models

The key discussion should cover the quality of the benchmark, confounding factors, product mix, pass-on, lingering effects, and uncertainty intervals.

### Causal inference and identification

When proposing fixed effects, instrumental variables, matching, regression discontinuity, or another design, answer four questions:

1. What variation identifies the effect?
2. Why is that variation plausibly exogenous?
3. What would violate the identifying assumption?
4. Which falsification or robustness checks can reveal a problem?

### Machine learning

Position machine learning carefully:

- Useful for prediction, classification, flexible controls, document review, anomaly detection, and exploration of high-dimensional data.
- It does not automatically identify causal effects.
- Explainability, validation, leakage, overfitting, class imbalance, and reproducibility matter in contested settings.
- The method must be transparent enough for review by colleagues and, where necessary, authorities or courts.

### Stata and reproducible workflow

Be ready to describe a project workflow rather than list commands:

1. Preserve raw data and create documented cleaning scripts.
2. Check units, duplicates, missing values, merges, time coverage, and outliers.
3. Build a clear analysis dataset with reproducible variable construction.
4. Produce descriptive tables and graphics before modelling.
5. Estimate a baseline specification, then pre-defined robustness variants.
6. Export consistent tables and figures and maintain a log of assumptions and changes.
7. Ask a colleague to review both the economic logic and the code.

If asked about Python versus Stata:

> Python is my strongest current environment for computational modelling, machine learning, and automation. I have also used Stata for econometric work for a course called Labor Econometrics, dealing with panel data analysis including fixed effect and random effect models, and understand why it is standard in applied competition projects. I am comfortable working in the team's preferred environment and transferring the same reproducibility and validation principles across tools.

One sentence to cover fixed effect vs. random effect:
> In panel data analysis, fixed effects control for time-invariant unobserved heterogeneity by allowing each entity to have its own intercept, while random effects assume this heterogeneity is uncorrelated with the regressors and can be captured as a random component.



### Interpreting statistical results

Always separate:

- Statistical significance from economic significance
- Association from causal interpretation
- Precision from validity
- A model's in-sample fit from out-of-sample or counterfactual reliability

In an interview case, translate coefficients into magnitudes a client can understand.
